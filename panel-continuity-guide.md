# Before you draw: track what changes between comic panels

Created by GenToon (Fairrium), Seoul, October 9, 2026. Maker disclosure: GenToon makes comic-creation software. The exercise below works with paper, drawing apps, and other comic editors as well as GenToon.

A scene can make sense in prose and become confusing in pictures. A sentence can say that someone removed a label, handed over a replacement, and attached it. A single panel needs a visible moment. If the drawing tries to show all three actions at once, the hands and objects may tell different stories.

Before illustrating, write down what each panel changes. Our free [Scene-to-Panels and Continuity Kit](https://github.com/gentoonaws/comic-creator-worksheets) provides editable text worksheets and a filled fictional example. There is no software account required to use them.

## Give the scene one visible destination

In the kit's original scene, Nora and Eli repair a blue lantern's route label. It begins with “NORTH PATH” and ends with “RIVER PATH.” That gives the reader something concrete to follow. The festival matters to the characters, but drawing a crowd would not help explain this repair.

Write the beginning and ending in two short lines. Then list only the information a reader needs to understand the change. For this scene, that includes the old label, the replacement, the map, and Eli's muddy boots: the boots explain the final joke.

## Separate a character anchor from a temporary state

Nora's glasses and curly hair are identity anchors. Her knowledge of the route is a state that changes. Eli's braid helps identify him, while the new label in his right hand explains the action.

Keep two columns in your notes: “recognize this person” and “true right now.” This makes review more precise. A different apron might be a visual mismatch; a character suddenly knowing the new route without seeing the map is a story mismatch. Both need attention, but the correction is different.

## Assign each panel a job

Try this four-moment plan:

| Moment | What the reader sees | What must carry forward |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | Eli arrives with a replacement label beside Nora's lantern | Old label attached; new label in Eli's right hand |
| Explanation | Eli points to the changed map | His left hand points; the right still holds the label |
| Repair | Nora peels the old label while Eli steadies the lantern | Left hand steadies; replacement remains available |
| Result | Corrected lantern; Eli looks down at his muddy boots | New label attached; old label discarded; boots visible |

These are planned moments, not finished images. Four panels are a starting constraint. If a reader cannot infer the handover and attachment between the last two moments, add a panel. A shorter comic is not helpful if its main action becomes a puzzle.

## Review the output against the plan

After drawing or generating, check objects before judging style. Which hand holds the label? Is the lantern still on the same table? Does the final crop show the boots? Read the bubbles in their visible order, then inspect lettering at the intended reading size. A good-looking close-up can still hide the fact that makes a joke work.

## Close with facts, then propose the next scene

At the lantern scene's accepted ending, the new label is attached, the old one lies on the table, and Eli's boots remain muddy. The lantern has not been delivered. “Carry it to the river path” is a possible next scene, not something that has already happened.

Record that distinction in the continuity sheet. It gives the next writer or illustrator a clear starting point and prevents a proposal from quietly becoming an established event.
